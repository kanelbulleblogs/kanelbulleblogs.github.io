If not for the office gossips and the who's dating who in Bollywood, Amma - my maternal aunt likes to narrate stories from the mythologies and I remember mumma doing the same with me. 
Growing up reading the Amar Chitra Katha, sometimes backed by my mumma's animated narrations - peculiar voices reserved for the asuras (*demons*) and the devas (*divine beings*), as she read their parts out loud. <br>


Cut to present, the other day Amma was heartily telling me the legend of Goddess Meenakshi, from a documentary she watched (well now I know where l got that bug from, perhaps runs in the family).
The documentary is produced by the HistoryTv and is on YouTube if you want to give a go, and features the Meenakshi Amman Temple's ***Chithirai*** (or the Hindu calendar month of *Chaitra* ) festival. It's about an hour long with really captivating visuals and an engaging direction. 
The  ***Chithirai*** festival is about the rituals surrounding the wedding of Lord Sundareswara (***Shiva***) and Goddess Meenakshi (***Shakti*** / ***Parvathy***), however what enchanted me the most was the temple’s striking Pandyan architecture[^1]. <br>

The temple complex is huge with architectural precisions and geometric principles unfathomable to a majority of the modern day infrastructure projects. A hallway of about a thousand columns or pillars cut from a single stone perfectly symmetrical and aligned, with exquisitely carved stone sculptures adorning the temple walls,
not to forget the tall grand Gopurams[^2] ornate with various depictions of gods and mythical figures . ***Yalis*** or ***azhi*** (*Tamil*) of them, were the ones which intrigued me the most. <br>

Picture a being with the body of a lion but head of an elephant and wings of a bird - that's the *yali* for you, otherwise known as the temple guardians . It doesn't actually conforms to any concrete living form. *Yazhis* are usually found at the entrance of the temples since they are regarded as temple guardians,
mostly in the southern parts of India. They can also be spotted in certain temples of South east Asia, especially Thailand and Cambodia in temples with influences of Buddhist - Hindu architecture.
And that’s precisely ***liminality*** for you (personified?) - the ambiguity of neither this nor that. <br>

I believe my earliest introduction to liminality was when I was still a kid, this one mythological story mumma told me - one of ***Prahlad*** (a devotee of ***Lord Vishnu***). I won’t go into the specifics of it but I want to highlight this boon which his father, the demon king ***Hiranyakashyap*** sought - <br>
<br>
>That no being , a human or an animal could kill him  
>That there would come no time , in the day or night when he would be killed  
>That there would be no place, on earth or in the sky where he would be killed  
>That no weapon, of any known elements be the reason for his death    

<br>
Smart, eh ? Playing well into the ambiguities
<br>
<br>

> But then he was outsmarted, out witted by ***Lord Vishnu*** who manifested himself as ***Narsimha*** ( Sanskrit : *nar* - human , *simha* - lion), neither a man nor an animal.  
> The demon king was killed at twilight, which was neither the day or the night,  
> On the threshold of his house (traditionally houses in India will have a low wall / stone edge separating the indoors of a house from the outdoors , more like a doorstep) , so yeah neither the indoors nor the outdoors  
> He was killed while on Narsimha’s lap , a place neither on the earth nor in the sky, with his stomach ripped apart with bare fingernails (no weapons)  

Feels like those loopholes you might leverage in a prenup, right ?<br>

Okay liminality, focus !<br>

We have been long conditioned to acknowledge things as black or white, right or wrong, or you know - things which fit right in the eyes of established societal (patriarchal?) norms - the age old dilemma of fitting in a box and then there would be times, times when we find ourselves not aligning with them - and we ***spiral***. 
I couldn't help but wonder how perfectly this complements that there is ***an action in inaction too*** - the classic quandary.)<br><br>
***Yali*** breaks free from this notion - acknowledging the fact that at some given point in time, we all are going to struggle in spaces ambiguous, challenging the realities and doubting our own selves but then that's perfectly fine - if not for them ***liminal spaces*** how exactly are we going to think outside the box ?<br>


Isn't it absolutely breath-taking though, a blissful pause at dawn while we eventually find ourselves at the day ?<br>
<br>
Or being tucked away in a good night's sleep right into the night, after a momentary lapse at dusk ?<br>
<br>
